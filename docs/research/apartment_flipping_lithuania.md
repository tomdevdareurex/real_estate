# Apartment Flipping in Lithuania

## Cap Rates, Renovation Budgets, Breakeven Analysis, Risks, and Comparison with the S&P 500

**Prepared:** 17 August 2026  
**Geographic focus:** Lithuania, using global real-estate investing principles

---

## Executive Summary

Apartment flipping is not primarily a renovation business. It is an **acquisition-pricing, valuation, project-management, financing, tax, and resale-liquidity business**.

The most important conclusions are:

1. A higher cap rate does **not** automatically make an apartment easier to sell.
2. Cap rate is primarily relevant to income-producing properties and investor buyers.
3. For a flip, the renovation budget should be tied mainly to the **conservative after-renovation value** and the **value created**, not only to the purchase price.
4. A useful first-project guideline is to keep renovation plus contingency below approximately **15% of conservative resale value** and below approximately **60% to 70% of the value uplift created by the renovation**.
5. The project's total cost should preferably remain below approximately **85% to 88% of conservative resale value**.
6. The deal should remain solvent if the resale price is 7% lower, renovation costs are 15% higher, and the sale is delayed by three months.
7. Flipping can outperform the S&P 500, but it involves concentrated risk, illiquidity, leverage, substantial work, and uncertain costs. It is not a like-for-like comparison with passive investing.
8. In flipping, most of the profit is normally secured when purchasing the property, not when choosing expensive finishes.

# 1. Does a Higher Cap Rate Make an Apartment Easier to Sell?

## Short answer

**Not necessarily.**

The capitalization rate is calculated as:

```text
Cap rate = Annual net operating income / Property value
```

Net operating income, or NOI, generally means rental income after vacancy and operating expenses, but before mortgage payments and personal income tax.

Cap rate is mainly a valuation shortcut for income-producing property. It is not a direct measure of resale liquidity, renovation profitability, or total investment return.

### Example

Assume an apartment has:

- Annual gross rent: €10,800
- Vacancy and operating expenses: €2,300
- Annual NOI: €8,500
- Market value: €170,000

```text
Cap rate = €8,500 / €170,000 = 5.0%
```

If the same apartment produced the same NOI but cost €140,000:

```text
Cap rate = €8,500 / €140,000 = 6.1%
```

The cheaper apartment has a higher cap rate, but that does not necessarily mean it is easier to resell.

## Why a high cap rate can indicate risk

A high cap rate can be attractive to a landlord because the property generates more income relative to its price. However, it can also reflect:

- A weaker location
- Lower tenant demand
- Greater vacancy risk
- Deferred maintenance
- Inferior building quality
- High operating expenses
- Unstable income
- Poor energy efficiency
- Limited appreciation prospects
- A smaller buyer pool

Therefore, a high-cap-rate apartment may actually be **harder to sell** if the yield is compensation for higher risk.

## What matters to different buyers

### Owner-occupiers

Owner-occupiers usually prioritize:

- Location
- Layout
- Natural light
- Condition
- Energy efficiency
- Monthly utility and heating costs
- Building quality
- Parking
- Balcony
- Mortgage affordability
- Emotional appeal

### Rental investors

Rental investors usually prioritize:

- Sustainable net rent
- Vacancy risk
- Tenant demand
- Operating expenses
- Net yield or cap rate
- Future maintenance requirements
- Exit liquidity

### Other flippers

Another flipper usually prioritizes:

- Discount to current market value
- Discount to potential after-renovation value
- Renovation complexity
- Time required
- Legal and construction risks
- Resale liquidity

## Practical conclusion

For apartment flipping, cap rate should be treated as a **secondary exit test**.

For example:

> If the renovated apartment does not sell quickly to an owner-occupier, would it still be attractive to a rental investor at the proposed selling price?

A reasonable rental yield can broaden the buyer pool, but cap rate alone will not make the apartment liquid.

# 2. The Correct Economic Model for an Apartment Flip

A common beginner calculation is:

```text
Profit = Selling price - Purchase price - Renovation
```

This is incomplete and can materially overstate profit.

The complete model is:

```text
Net flip profit =
    Sale price
  - Purchase price
  - Purchase costs
  - Renovation cost
  - Renovation contingency
  - Financing costs
  - Holding costs
  - Selling costs
  - Taxes
```

## 2.1 Acquisition costs

Include:

1. Purchase price
2. Notary costs
3. Registration costs
4. Legal due diligence
5. Technical inspection
6. Property valuation
7. Mortgage establishment and bank fees
8. Buyer's agent fees, if applicable
9. Documentation corrections
10. Costs relating to occupants, ownership issues, liens, or encumbrances

Lithuania generally has lower acquisition friction than countries with large property transfer taxes, but the transaction is not cost-free.

## 2.2 Renovation costs

Include:

1. Design and planning
2. Demolition
3. Waste removal
4. Electrical wiring
5. Plumbing
6. Heating-related work
7. Ventilation
8. Bathroom
9. Kitchen
10. Flooring
11. Wall and ceiling preparation
12. Painting
13. Windows
14. Interior and exterior doors
15. Lighting
16. Appliances
17. Furniture and staging items
18. Construction permits and documentation
19. Project management
20. Final cleaning
21. Defect correction
22. Contingency

## 2.3 Financing and holding costs

Include:

- Loan interest
- Origination and bank fees
- Insurance
- Utilities
- Heating
- Building administration fees
- Property tax, where applicable
- Security
- Maintenance during the holding period
- Opportunity cost of invested equity

## 2.4 Selling costs

Include:

- Broker commission
- Photography
- Floor plans
- Marketing
- Staging
- Legal and notarial costs borne by the seller
- Negotiation discount
- Buyer-requested repairs
- Post-inspection concessions
- Taxes

## 2.5 Your time

Your own labour has economic value. Track hours spent on:

- Finding the property
- Analysing comparable sales
- Negotiating
- Arranging financing
- Selecting contractors
- Purchasing materials
- Managing the project
- Inspecting work
- Marketing
- Conducting viewings
- Completing the sale

A flip that produces €15,000 after tax but takes 500 hours of work has generated only €30 per hour before adjusting for investment risk.

# 3. Renovation Cost Levels in Lithuania

Indicative screening ranges for Lithuanian apartment renovation are approximately:

| Renovation level | Indicative cost per m² |
|---|---:|
| Cosmetic | €150 to €250 |
| Partial | €300 to €450 |
| Full | €500 to €800 |
| Premium | €800 to €1,200+ |

These are only preliminary market estimates. Actual prices depend on:

- City and neighbourhood
- Apartment size
- Building age
- Contractor availability
- Material standard
- Existing condition
- Layout changes
- Electrical and plumbing condition
- Heritage restrictions
- Required permits
- Project timeline

Older buildings can require a surcharge because of unknown wiring, deteriorated plumbing, moisture, uneven walls and floors, ventilation problems, or other concealed defects.

For each potential acquisition, obtain at least two or three itemized contractor estimates before making an unconditional commitment whenever possible.

# 4. Renovation Breakeven

There are two useful definitions of breakeven.

## 4.1 Renovation-only breakeven

First estimate the value created by the renovation:

```text
Renovation value created =
    Conservative renovated value
  - Current unrenovated market value
```

The renovation economically breaks even when:

```text
Renovation value created >=
    Renovation cost
  + Incremental holding costs
  + Incremental financing costs
  + Incremental selling costs
  + Incremental tax effects
```

### Example

Assume:

- Current unrenovated value: €135,000
- Conservative renovated value: €170,000
- Gross value uplift: €35,000
- Renovation: €24,000
- Contingency: €3,000
- Additional holding and financing: €2,500
- Additional selling and tax effects: €2,000

```text
Economic renovation gain =
€35,000 - €24,000 - €3,000 - €2,500 - €2,000
= €3,500
```

The renovation technically creates a positive return, but €3,500 is not sufficient compensation for valuation risk, delays, defects, and project-management work.

## 4.2 Whole-project breakeven sale price

If some selling costs vary with the sale price, use:

```text
Breakeven sale price =
All costs before variable selling costs
/
(1 - Variable selling-cost rate)
```

### Example

| Cost item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Purchase price | €115,000 |
| Purchase and legal costs | €2,500 |
| Renovation | €27,000 |
| Renovation contingency | €3,500 |
| Financing and holding | €5,000 |
| Fixed resale costs | €1,000 |
| Total before variable selling fee | €154,000 |

If variable selling expenses equal 3% of the selling price:

```text
Breakeven sale price = €154,000 / (1 - 0.03)
                     = €158,763
```

If the target profit is €20,000 before income tax:

```text
Required sale price = (€154,000 + €20,000) / (1 - 0.03)
                    = €179,381
```

This shows why the difference between purchase price and resale price is not the same as profit.

# 5. Rules of Thumb for the Maximum Renovation Budget

There is no universal limit based solely on the purchase price. The better reference points are:

1. Conservative after-renovation value
2. Value uplift created by the renovation
3. Total project cost
4. Expected profit margin
5. Downside scenario

## 5.1 Renovation as a percentage of expected selling price

Initial screening ranges:

| Renovation type | Renovation as percentage of conservative selling price |
|---|---:|
| Cosmetic | 5% to 10% |
| Partial | 10% to 15% |
| Full | 15% to 25% |
| Above 25% | High risk and requires exceptional acquisition pricing |

These are screening guidelines, not formal Lithuanian industry standards.

## 5.2 Renovation as a percentage of purchase price

A secondary screening test is:

| Renovation type | Renovation as percentage of purchase price |
|---|---:|
| Cosmetic | 5% to 10% |
| Partial | 10% to 20% |
| Full | 20% to 35% |
| Above 35% | High risk and requires detailed justification |

This ratio can be misleading because the same renovation budget may create very different value in two apartments purchased for the same price.

### Example

Two apartments both cost €120,000 and require a €20,000 renovation.

- Apartment A can sell for €150,000 after renovation.
- Apartment B can sell for €180,000 after renovation.

The renovation is 16.7% of purchase price in both cases, but Apartment B has much stronger economics.

## 5.3 Renovation relative to the value uplift

This is a more useful rule:

> **Renovation plus contingency should preferably consume no more than approximately 60% to 70% of the conservative value uplift.**

The remaining 30% to 40% of the uplift must help cover:

- Financing
- Holding costs
- Selling costs
- Tax effects
- Cost overruns
- Market risk
- Your time
- Profit

### Acceptable example

- Unrenovated value: €130,000
- Conservative renovated value: €170,000
- Value uplift: €40,000
- Renovation: €24,000

```text
Renovation / Value uplift = €24,000 / €40,000 = 60%
```

This passes the initial screen, although all other costs still need to be modelled.

### Weak example

- Unrenovated value: €130,000
- Conservative renovated value: €160,000
- Value uplift: €30,000
- Renovation: €27,000

```text
Renovation / Value uplift = €27,000 / €30,000 = 90%
```

You would spend €27,000 to create only €30,000 of estimated value. A small cost overrun or price reduction could eliminate the entire economic gain.

## 5.4 Recommended combined rule for a first project

For a first Lithuanian apartment flip, use all four requirements:

### Requirement 1: Renovation versus resale value

```text
Renovation including contingency
/
Conservative resale value
<= approximately 15%
```

Up to approximately 20% may be defensible for a genuine full renovation if the apartment was purchased with a large discount.

### Requirement 2: Renovation versus value created

```text
Renovation including contingency
/
Conservative value uplift
<= approximately 60% to 70%
```

Lower is better.

### Requirement 3: Total project cost versus resale value

```text
Purchase price + All project costs
/
Conservative resale value
<= approximately 85% to 88%
```

This corresponds to an expected pre-tax margin of approximately 12% to 15% of the selling price.

### Requirement 4: Stress-tested survival

The flip should remain above breakeven if:

- Selling price is 7% below the base case
- Renovation is 15% above budget
- Completion and sale are delayed by three months
- Broker commission is required
- Financing costs increase
- A meaningful tax liability applies

## 5.5 Renovation contingency

Use at least:

- **10%:** very simple cosmetic project with well-documented condition
- **15%:** normal renovation
- **20%:** old apartment with uncertain wiring, plumbing, moisture, structure, or documentation

The contingency must be included in the acquisition decision. It is not an optional reserve added after work begins.

# 6. The Global 70% Rule

A widely used international screening rule is:

```text
Maximum allowable purchase price =
70% x After-repair value
- Renovation cost
```

### Example

- After-repair value: €180,000
- Renovation: €30,000

```text
Maximum purchase price =
0.70 x €180,000 - €30,000
= €96,000
```

The remaining 30% is not pure profit. It is intended to cover:

- Acquisition costs
- Financing
- Holding expenses
- Selling costs
- Unexpected costs
- Taxes
- Profit

## Limitations of the 70% rule

The rule is only a rapid filter. It does not replace a complete financial model.

It may be too conservative when:

- Transaction costs are low
- Renovation is cosmetic and predictable
- The sale can be completed quickly
- Resale comparables are very reliable
- The property is in an exceptionally liquid market

It may be insufficiently conservative when:

- Renovation is complex
- The building is old
- Financing is expensive
- The buyer pool is small
- The after-repair value is uncertain
- Tax treatment is unfavourable
- The holding period can be long

For a first project, do not weaken the rule merely because Lithuanian transaction costs may be lower than US transaction costs.

# 7. A Worked Lithuanian Example

Assume a 50 m² apartment:

| Item | Base estimate |
|---|---:|
| Purchase price | €110,000 |
| Purchase costs | €2,000 |
| Renovation | €25,000 |
| Contingency | €3,750 |
| Financing and holding | €4,500 |
| Selling costs | €5,000 |
| Total project cost | €150,250 |
| Conservative sale price | €175,000 |
| Pre-tax profit | €24,750 |

## Ratios

### Renovation including contingency versus purchase price

```text
€28,750 / €110,000 = 26.1%
```

### Renovation including contingency versus selling price

```text
€28,750 / €175,000 = 16.4%
```

### Total project cost versus selling price

```text
€150,250 / €175,000 = 85.9%
```

### Pre-tax profit margin

```text
€24,750 / €175,000 = 14.1%
```

This project may be viable, although the renovation budget is substantial relative to the resale value.

## Stress case

Reduce the expected selling price by 7%:

```text
€175,000 x 93% = €162,750
```

Increase the original €25,000 renovation budget by 15%:

```text
Additional renovation cost = €25,000 x 15% = €3,750
```

Approximate stressed project cost:

```text
€150,250 + €3,750 = €154,000
```

Approximate stressed pre-tax profit:

```text
€162,750 - €154,000 = €8,750
```

The project survives the stress scenario, but the downside profit is much less attractive. Taxes and additional delay costs could reduce it further.

# 8. What to Analyse in the Lithuanian Market

## 8.1 Micro-location

Analyse at street and building level:

- Walking distance to public transport
- Access to employment centres
- Universities and schools
- Shops and services
- Traffic and noise
- Parking availability
- Green areas
- Neighbourhood reputation
- Nearby development pipeline
- Competing new construction
- Rental demand
- Owner-occupier demand

A city-wide average price per square metre is not enough. Two apartments in the same district can have different liquidity because of the street, building, floor, orientation, noise, or parking.

## 8.2 Building quality

A newly renovated apartment can still be difficult to sell if the building is unattractive or financially troubled.

Check:

- Roof
- Facade
- Foundation
- Stairwell
- Lift
- Heating system
- Energy performance
- Electrical risers
- Water and sewage risers
- Ventilation
- Planned capital works
- Building-renovation loans
- Homeowners' association disputes
- Owner arrears
- Insurance history
- Heritage restrictions

## 8.3 Legal and cadastral conformity

Verify:

- The physical layout matches the registered plan
- Any previous layout changes were legal
- Balconies and extensions are registered
- The premises have the correct legal designation
- There are no liens or unexpected usage rights
- There are no ownership disputes
- Planned wall changes are permitted
- Wet areas can legally and technically be moved
- Utility accounts are settled
- Building obligations are documented

Unauthorized work can produce fines, legalization costs, delays, financing problems, and difficulty selling the apartment.

## 8.4 After-renovation value

The after-renovation value, or ARV, should be based on completed sales where possible, not only asking prices.

Use comparables with similar:

- Building or nearby location
- Size
- Number of rooms
- Floor
- Lift access
- Balcony
- Parking
- Condition
- Energy efficiency
- Construction period
- Layout

Prefer transactions from the previous three to six months. Adjust for differences conservatively.

Asking prices show current competition, but they do not prove the final price buyers will pay.

## 8.5 Renovation value ceiling

Do not renovate above the standard supported by the local buyer pool.

A premium renovation is not automatically rewarded in a price-sensitive neighbourhood. Install the quality level expected by the target buyer, not the quality level you personally prefer.

Improvements that often create value include:

- Better and legal layout
- Functional kitchen
- Modern bathroom
- Good lighting
- Neutral and consistent flooring
- Storage
- Reliable plumbing and electricity
- Clean documentation
- Energy and heating improvements where feasible

# 9. Target Profit and Return Requirements

For a first project, a possible discipline framework is:

1. Expected pre-tax profit of at least **€20,000 to €25,000**
2. Expected profit margin of at least **12% to 15% of resale price**
3. Expected return on cash invested of at least **20%**, subject to a credible timeline
4. Positive profit under the downside stress scenario

These are not official Lithuanian standards. They are practical hurdles designed to compensate for concentration, illiquidity, construction uncertainty, taxation, and the investor's labour.

A fixed minimum profit is important because a 15% return on a very small project can still be inadequate compensation for the work and risk.

# 10. Lithuanian Tax Considerations

Lithuanian tax authorities generally calculate taxable real-estate sale income with reference to sale proceeds, acquisition cost, and qualifying mandatory acquisition or disposal payments. Progressive personal income-tax rates can become relevant depending on annual taxable non-employment income.

Do not automatically assume that every renovation receipt will reduce taxable profit in the same way that it reduces economic profit.

Before the first purchase, obtain professional clarification on:

1. Whether repeated purchases and sales could be treated as business or economic activity
2. Which renovation expenses increase recognized acquisition cost
3. Required invoices and payment evidence
4. Potential VAT consequences
5. The consequences of operating as an individual
6. The consequences of registered individual activity
7. The use of a small partnership or limited company
8. Social contribution implications
9. Profit distribution and extraction
10. Property-tax consequences during the holding period

Tax should be integrated into the financial model before purchasing, not considered only when the apartment is sold.

# 11. Can Flipping Be More Profitable Than Investing in the S&P 500?

Yes, a successful flip can outperform the S&P 500 on cash invested, particularly when leverage and active work create value. However, this does not necessarily make flipping the better investment.

Historically, broad US equities have generated approximately 10% nominal annualized returns over very long periods with dividends reinvested, with substantial variation across individual years and decades. A long-term investor must tolerate market drawdowns, but does not need to manage contractors, construction defects, legal documentation, or property sales.

## Comparison

| Apartment flipping | S&P 500 index investment |
|---|---|
| Concentrated in one property and location | Diversified across many large companies |
| Illiquid | Normally liquid on trading days |
| High transaction and operational friction | Low transaction friction |
| Requires active sourcing and management | Can be almost completely passive |
| Can use property-specific leverage | Retail investors normally invest without leverage |
| Skill can create additional return | Return is primarily market-driven |
| Construction, legal, tax, and sale risks | Market, valuation, and currency risks |
| Returns are lumpy | Returns are continuously observable but volatile |
| High time commitment | Very low time commitment |

## Annualizing a flip return

Use:

```text
Annualized flip return =
(1 + After-tax profit / Average cash invested)^(12 / Months held)
- 1
```

### Example

Assume:

- Average cash invested: €70,000
- After-tax profit: €14,000
- Holding period: 9 months

```text
Annualized return =
(1 + €14,000 / €70,000)^(12 / 9) - 1
≈ 27.5%
```

This appears attractive. However, if the project required 400 hours of work and those hours were worth €10,000 to €20,000, part of the return was compensation for labour rather than return on capital.

## Correct comparison

Compare the flip with the S&P 500 after adjusting for:

- Tax
- Leverage
- Hours worked
- Liquidity
- Concentration
- Downside risk
- Transaction costs
- Reinvestment delay between projects
- Currency exposure

Flipping should be evaluated partly as a business activity, not purely as a passive investment.

# 12. Recommended First-Project Profile

For a first Lithuanian project, a lower-complexity target would usually have:

- A small or medium-sized apartment
- Standard and efficient layout
- Liquid Vilnius or Kaunas submarket
- Strong owner-occupier demand
- Secondary rental-investor demand
- No structural-wall changes
- No major wet-area relocation
- Clear cadastral documentation
- A reasonably maintained building
- No major association disputes
- No uncertain occupant situation
- A cosmetic or moderate renovation
- At least three credible resale comparables
- Multiple contractor quotations

Avoid depending on aggressive market appreciation. The project should work at today's conservative value.

# 13. Deal-Analysis Checklist

## Market and exit

- [ ] Define the target buyer
- [ ] Collect at least three strong comparables
- [ ] Distinguish listing prices from completed sale prices
- [ ] Estimate a conservative resale value
- [ ] Check rental yield as a secondary exit route
- [ ] Estimate realistic days to sell
- [ ] Identify competing renovated apartments and new developments

## Legal and building

- [ ] Inspect cadastral plans
- [ ] Confirm actual layout matches registration
- [ ] Check title and encumbrances
- [ ] Check building association minutes
- [ ] Check planned capital works
- [ ] Check building debts and renovation loans
- [ ] Confirm permit requirements
- [ ] Inspect plumbing, wiring, moisture, ventilation, roof, and facade risks

## Renovation

- [ ] Prepare an itemized scope of work
- [ ] Obtain two or three contractor quotes
- [ ] Separate labour and material costs
- [ ] Include appliances and furniture
- [ ] Include demolition and waste removal
- [ ] Include design, permits, and cleaning
- [ ] Add 15% to 20% contingency where appropriate
- [ ] Set a completion schedule with milestones

## Finance and tax

- [ ] Include acquisition costs
- [ ] Include loan interest and fees
- [ ] Include utilities and holding expenses
- [ ] Include broker and selling costs
- [ ] Include tax estimate
- [ ] Confirm renovation-expense documentation requirements
- [ ] Value your own time

## Decision tests

- [ ] Renovation plus contingency is preferably no more than 15% of resale value
- [ ] Renovation plus contingency is preferably no more than 60% to 70% of value uplift
- [ ] Total project cost is preferably no more than 85% to 88% of resale value
- [ ] Expected pre-tax margin reaches approximately 12% to 15%
- [ ] Base-case profit is at least €20,000 to €25,000
- [ ] Project remains above breakeven after stress testing

# 14. Final Decision Rule

The most useful question is not:

> How much can I spend on renovation relative to the purchase price?

The better question is:

> How many euros of conservative resale value will each renovation euro create, after all additional costs and taxes?

A €20,000 renovation that conservatively creates €35,000 to €40,000 of value may work.

A €10,000 renovation that creates only €11,000 of value is usually unattractive, even though the budget appears small.

## Suggested first-project rule

> Keep renovation plus contingency below approximately 15% of conservative resale value and below approximately 65% of the estimated renovation-created value. Require total project cost to remain below approximately 85% to 88% of conservative resale value, and verify that the project survives a 7% resale-price decline, a 15% renovation overrun, and a three-month delay.

Most importantly:

> **You normally make the profit when you buy the apartment, not when you renovate it.**
